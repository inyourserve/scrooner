# 42 — Why 56.85% Isn't Higher, and a Multi-Parser Plan to Reach 70%+

> **Status:** Living plan, started 2026-09-03 — a direct request to stop treating coverage as a series of tag-by-tag patches and instead do an extensive, root-caused study of why the "should be mandatory" financial statement items aren't near 100%, then design a plan (multiple independent parsers, not one, in a continuous improvement loop) to close it. Complements, doesn't replace, doc 41 (the score itself and the tag-by-tag ranked list). **Owner:** Founder/Product · **Review:** whenever a parser service or a root-cause finding changes.

## Part 1 — Why the mandatory GAAP items aren't at 100% (real root causes, not assumed)

Every operating company's 10-K/10-Q *legally must* include an income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement (Reg S-X). If a core line item is missing, one of exactly three things is true: (a) the company genuinely doesn't have that line (a pre-revenue biotech has no revenue), (b) the company's *statement structure* doesn't map onto the generic template we chose (a bank's balance sheet isn't classified into current/non-current), or (c) our own tag mapping has a real gap. Sampled every "should be near-100%" concept's actual missing companies by SIC code — not assumed — to tell these apart.

| Concept | Coverage | Dominant cause of the gap, by real company count |
|---|---|---|
| `current_assets`/`current_liabilities` | 82.1%/82.3% | **(b) Structural.** 176 REITs + 174+83 banks + 95 brokers + 54 insurers + 36+32 savings institutions + 28 investment advisers ≈ 700+ of ~930 missing companies. Banks/insurers/REITs present an **unclassified balance sheet by liquidity**, not current/non-current — this is a real, permitted GAAP presentation choice for financial institutions (ASC 210), not a filer being sloppy. |
| `operating_income` | 84.5% | **(b) Structural**, same population — banks/insurers routinely have no clean "operating income" subtotal; their income statement starts from net interest income or premium revenue and moves through a different expense structure. 169+78 banks + 74 brokers + 54 REITs + 42 insurers ≈ 500+ of ~810 missing. |
| `income_before_tax` | 84.2% | Same financial-institution population, same reason. |
| `revenue` | 87.25% (up from 86.3% same day) | **(a) Legitimate absence**, mostly: 280 SPACs ("Blank Checks"), 95 pre-revenue pharma + 35 biological products (clinical-stage biotech), 74 commodity brokers (trading gains, not "revenue"). Only ~8 remaining banks — today's tag fix already closed most of the real, closeable gap here. |
| `diluted_eps`/`basic_eps` | 91.5%/92.2% | Mix of (a) and (b): 168 SPACs (no meaningful EPS pre-merger), 94 commodity brokers, some financial institutions report EPS differently or not at all if structured as a trust/LP. |
| `income_tax_expense` | 88.5% | Mostly (a): companies with sustained losses often have no tax expense line, or REITs (pass-through entities, no corporate income tax by design — this is *the* defining REIT tax structure, not a gap). |
| `shares_outstanding` | 91.0% | **(c) Real, closeable gap** — see Part 1b below, this is the one with genuine remaining upside from better extraction, not structural exclusion. |
| `total_liabilities`, `stockholders_equity`, `cash_and_equivalents`, `cfo`, `total_assets`, `net_income` | 90.9%–99.7% | Already close to ceiling — remaining gap is almost entirely SPACs/very recently registered shells with incomplete filing history, genuinely not fixable by better parsing. |

**The headline finding: for `current_assets`/`current_liabilities`/`operating_income` specifically, the honest ceiling with the current single-template approach is roughly 80-85%, not 100%** — because ~15-18% of the active population is a bank, insurer, REIT, or broker-dealer whose real financial statements don't have those line items *at all*, under any parser. Treating that gap as "unclosed" the same way a real tag-mapping miss is unclosed would be dishonest — the fix isn't a better parser for the *same* concept, it's recognizing these companies need a **sector-specific equivalent concept**, covered in Part 3.

## Part 1b — `shares_outstanding`: the one core concept with real, closeable upside

Unlike the structural gaps above, `shares_outstanding` (91.0%) has a genuine, demonstrated fix path already partly built (`company_master/shares_outstanding_fallback.py`) but never scaled past 2 companies, and — piloted today — only 23% successful on a random 30-company sample. This is Part 2's primary target.

## Part 2 — Multi-parser architecture (not one fallback service, several independent ones)

The explicit ask: don't build one parser and hope it generalizes. Every extraction method has blind spots; independent methods have *different* blind spots, so running more than one and merging results (same "prefer A, else B, else C" idiom this project already uses for `total_debt_resolved`) closes more real gaps than perfecting any single one.

### Parser 1 (existing, primary): XBRL tag-based `first_match`/`sum` resolution
`resolve.py` + `concept_mapping` — the foundation, works whenever a filer tagged *some* form of the concept, even under an unexpected tag name. This is what today's `revenue`/`comprehensive_income`/`research_and_development` fallback-tag additions extend. **Strength:** structured, reliable, zero parsing risk once a tag is verified safe. **Blind spot:** only works if the filer tagged the concept under *any* known name — companies using genuinely novel or company-specific extension tags, or omitting the concept from XBRL entirely (common in older/smaller filers), are invisible to it no matter how many tag variants get added.

### Parser 2 (exists, needs generalizing): cover-page / narrative text extraction
`company_master/shares_outstanding_fallback.py` — regex extraction from a 10-K's own cover page. **Strength:** works even when XBRL tagging is entirely absent, since cover-page share counts are a mandatory disclosure regardless of XBRL completeness. **Blind spot:** today's 30-company pilot (23% success) confirms the regex was validated against only 2 companies' phrasing and doesn't generalize — needs real, iterative pattern-widening (Part 4's loop) before being trusted at scale.

### Parser 3 (exists elsewhere, not yet applied to core concepts): rendered-report table extraction
`segments/segment_revenue.py` already established this technique for segment data: every filing has an auto-rendered `R*.htm` "Details" report (discoverable via `FilingSummary.xml`) with the real financial-statement tables as parseable HTML — **independent of whether the underlying XBRL concept was tagged with a name this project recognizes**, since it reads the company's own as-filed presentation, not a specific tag string. **Not yet applied to any of the core mandatory concepts.** This is a real, structurally different extraction method from both Parser 1 (no tag-name dependency at all) and Parser 2 (reads the primary financial statements, not just the cover page) — worth building as a genuine third independent source, specifically targeted at the `current_assets`/`operating_income`-shaped gaps for companies that *do* have a classified balance sheet but under an unrecognized tag.

### Parser 4 (new, proposed): sector-specific concept profiles for financial institutions
Not a text-parsing technique — a **mapping-architecture** change. For the ~800-1000 financial institutions where `current_assets`/`operating_income`/`revenue` are structurally inapplicable (Part 1), build parallel, sector-scoped canonical concepts (e.g., `bank_total_interest_income` as a bank's revenue-equivalent, `bank_net_interest_and_noninterest_income` as its operating-income-equivalent) rather than leaving the generic concept null. This doesn't raise the *generic* concept's coverage (correctly — a bank's classified-balance-sheet field really doesn't exist), but it closes the *product* gap: a bank's company page and screener results shouldn't be blanker than an industrial company's just because the underlying concept template assumed a non-financial company. Real effort (needs its own tag research per sector — banks/insurers/REITs each have different natural equivalents), scoped as its own follow-up, not started this pass.

### How they combine
Same idiom as `total_debt_resolved` (doc 40) and the `first_match` priority chain already in place: Parser 1 first (cheapest, most reliable), Parser 3 second (same underlying filing, different extraction angle, no new fetch), Parser 2 third (cover-page only, narrower scope but zero-XBRL-dependency), never silently double-counted or allowed to override a higher-confidence source — each new parser is a new `provisional`-confidence fallback tier, verified the same coexistence-check way every tag added this week was verified, never trusted blind.

### Parser 2, generalized into a reusable framework (built 2026-09-03)

Generalizing `shares_outstanding_fallback.py` (2 validated companies → 9 real phrasings → 63.6% real success, `doc/learnings/2026-09-03-shares-outstanding-parser-generalization.md`) surfaced a real, reusable shape worth extracting rather than re-deriving per concept: `company_master/text_extraction.py` now provides a generic **head parser / sub-parser** framework.

- **A head parser owns one extraction target** (e.g. "shares outstanding from a 10-K cover page"). It holds an ordered list of **sub-parsers** — each an independently-tested `(regex, combine-function)` pair for one real phrasing, optionally gated (only tried under some condition, e.g. "no class/series mentioned anywhere") and optionally scale-hint-aware ("in thousands" nearby multiplies by 1,000).
- `run_head_parser()` tries each sub-parser in priority order (most structurally-specific first — a lesson paid for in real false positives this week: looser patterns crowd out correct ones when a document's own flattened-table layout creates coincidental adjacencies), applies a shared plausibility floor/ceiling, and returns `(value, which_sub_parser_matched)` — real telemetry: if a sub-parser never fires across a full-population run, it's a candidate to retire; if one fires constantly, it's the highest-value one to scrutinize for edge cases next.
- `shares_outstanding_fallback.py` was refactored onto this framework the same day (11 new framework tests plus all 16 original behavior tests passing unchanged, verified byte-identical against real data before and after) — proof the abstraction doesn't cost correctness, not just a paper design.

**Applying this to "almost all core metrics" needs one honest caveat first**: this framework is Parser 2/3's tool — it closes gaps where the real number exists in prose or a rendered table but isn't under a tag this project recognizes. It does **not** help the Part 1 structural gaps (`current_assets`/`operating_income`/`revenue` for banks/insurers/REITs) — those need Parser 4 (sector-specific concepts), a different kind of fix entirely, because the number genuinely isn't presented that way for those companies at all. Applying the framework where it doesn't fit would just produce a confidently-wrong result, not more coverage.

**Where the framework fits next, ranked by real leverage** (a head parser per target, reusing the same discipline: read real failures → write sub-parsers → test against real text → pilot on a fresh sample → verify → scale):

1. **Parser 3, generalized the same way**: `segments/segment_revenue.py`'s `R*.htm` rendered-table technique already proved the *data source*; it was never generalized into *this* framework's sub-parser shape. Doing so would let `revenue`/`operating_income`/`current_assets` gaps (for non-financial companies with real classified statements under unrecognized tags — a real subset of Part 1's population, not the structural majority) get the same systematic treatment. **Now the top-ranked target** — see the correction below.
2. **`employee_count`** (3.4%, structural — `business_text.py` already has its own bespoke extractor) — lower priority since it doesn't feed any locked metric, but a natural candidate to migrate onto the shared framework for consistency once it's proven out further.

**Correction, checked 2026-09-03 (doc 41 Priority 4)**: `dividends_per_share`/`dividends_paid` were originally ranked #1 here. Before building, checked *who* is actually missing `dividends_paid` by SIC code (the same reality check that found real structural ceilings for banks/`current_assets`) — the missing population is overwhelmingly growth-stage, non-dividend-paying industries (pharma, SPACs, software, biotech, mining exploration), very likely genuine correct nulls, not a text-parsing gap. **Deprioritized below Parser 3** for that reason — a dividends head parser would plausibly find real prose for only a small minority of the "missing" companies, since most of them simply never declared a dividend to disclose.

Each of these is real, scoped, moderate-effort work — not started this pass beyond the framework itself and the `shares_outstanding` proof.

## Part 3 — Realistic path to 70%, honestly reasoned

`avg_metric_coverage_score` is an **unweighted mean across all 64 metrics** — this matters for what "70%" actually requires. Some metrics have a real ceiling well under 70% no matter how good the underlying concept coverage gets:

- **Piotroski F-Score**: needs 9 simultaneous inputs × 2 consecutive years. Even at 95% coverage for every one of its 9 inputs, the joint probability compounds down (0.95^18 ≈ 40% *if* independent, likely lower in practice since gaps correlate by company type) — this metric may simply never clear 50%, and that's correct, not a bug.
- **`dividends_per_share`/`dividends_paid`-derived metrics** (`payout_ratio`, `dividend_growth_streak_years`, `dps_growth_*`): capped by how many of 5,216 companies pay dividends at all — not all real companies do, so 100% was never the right ceiling here either.
- **Price-dependent metrics**: capped by Alpaca's own real market coverage (currently unbounded to check further; likely has its own real ceiling below 100% for illiquid/newly-listed tickers).

**Given that, reaching 70% on the unweighted score requires the *fixable* metrics (the ones without a structural ceiling) to individually approach 85-95%, to offset the metrics that will legitimately stay below 50%.** That's an aggressive but not unreasonable target *if* Parts 1-2's multi-parser work lands well — the mandatory GAAP items are mostly already 80-99%, and the metrics built on them (margins, growth rates, ratios) inherit that ceiling once their own concept-level gaps close.

**Built 2026-09-03**: a second, core-scoped score, `avg_core_v1_metric_coverage_score`, tracked alongside the existing unweighted `avg_metric_coverage_score` — restricted to the 20 metric rows doc 02 actually locked for V1 (`CORE_V1_METRIC_NAMES` in `coverage_snapshot.py`, sourced directly from `mapper/definitions.py`, never a separately-maintained list that could drift). Purely additive — computing and storing it changes nothing about `avg_metric_coverage_score`'s own formula or meaning, both are tracked side by side. Result, verified against live data: **66.04%**, meaningfully higher than the full 57.19% average, confirming the metrics a real investor actually opens the product for are in materially better shape than the full 64-metric average alone suggested.

**Still an open product call, not decided by this build**: whether `avg_core_v1_metric_coverage_score` should become the *primary* number reported/optimized for going forward, with the full 64-metric average kept as a secondary/complete-picture figure, or whether both stay equally weighted in how progress gets reported. The scaffolding exists either way — this is a presentation/prioritization decision, not an engineering one.

## Part 4 — The continuous improvement loop (learnings-driven, not one-shot)

Formalizing the pattern this week's real work has already validated three separate times (R&D tags, `revenue` tag, the `shares_outstanding_fallback` pilot):

1. **Measure**: `scrooner-map snapshot-coverage`, cross-checked against live data (yesterday's anomaly means never trust the printed summary alone — always spot-verify).
2. **Sample failures by category**: for the lowest-coverage concept/parser, pull the real list of missing companies, group by SIC/sector — don't guess at the cause, look at who's actually missing.
3. **Root-cause each category**: structural (accept, document, move on) vs. real gap (candidate tag exists, or a parser needs a new pattern).
4. **Verify before scaling**: coexistence-check any new tag (does its value match the primary when both exist?); pilot any new parser on ~30 real companies before a full run (today's `shares_outstanding_fallback` pilot is the template — 23% success caught *before* burning the full population's SEC-fetch budget).
5. **Ship narrow, re-measure, repeat**: one concept or one parser generalization at a time, full-population rerun, fresh snapshot, zero-mismatch verification, before moving to the next item.
6. **Write down what was learned**, even (especially) the rejections — doc 40/41's per-candidate tables of "tried and correctly rejected" are as valuable as the successes, since they stop the same wrong candidate from being re-tried blind next time.

This loop is already running (it's exactly how today's work happened) — Part 4 exists to make it explicit and repeatable by name, not to introduce something new.
